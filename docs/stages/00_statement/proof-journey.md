## Level 1 — Abitur / First-Principles

To settle the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, the formalization does not hunt for individual zeros of {{concept:riemann-zeta-function|the global function $\zeta(s)$ extending $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^{-s}$}} or of {{concept:dirichlet-l-function|the family of twisted functions $L(\chi, s)$ built from periodic multiplicative weights $\chi(n)$}}. Instead, it works directly with how irregularly {{concept:prime-number|whole numbers greater than $1$ with no smaller positive divisors}} deviate from their smooth average.

A classical one-sided principle connects prime deviations to zeros in {{concept:complex-plane|the two-dimensional grid of points $s = \sigma + it$}}: if a carefully weighted prime-counting error term $R_{q_0}(x)$ stays bounded above by a constant times $x^{-1/2 + \varepsilon}$ for every small $\varepsilon > 0$ as $x \to \infty$, then no {{term:dirichlet-l-function}} (nor the {{term:riemann-zeta-function}}) can vanish anywhere to the right of {{concept:critical-line|the central vertical line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$}}. By symmetry across that line inside {{concept:critical-strip|the open vertical band $0 < \operatorname{Re}(s) < 1$}}, ruling out zeros on the right half forces every nontrivial zero onto the {{term:critical-line}}.

How can a proof force an upper bound on a prime-counting error term? The core engine constructs a finite-dimensional grid of algebraic objects — a matrix whose entries record both geometric sizes and prime-power divisibility — and traps the prime deviation inside a **three-way tension** between three theorems:

1. **Theorem A — Shortness (Upper Bound):** A family of carefully chosen "source" functions is lifted through a chain of $m \approx \sqrt{x}$ steps into a target space. Each derivative along the chain picks up guaranteed divisibility by prime powers proportional to the prime-counting sum, while its overall geometric size stays controlled. Dividing out that guaranteed prime divisibility shrinks the lifted vectors: the more primes pile up above expectation (making the remainder $R_{q_0}(x)$ large), the *shorter* these lifted vectors become.
2. **Theorem B — Enough Translates (Spanning):** A handful of short vectors alone could all point in nearly the same direction and hide inside a tiny subspace. Theorem B proves that a small number of shifted copies of the lifted source functions spread out cleanly and span almost the entire target space, leaving almost no room for degenerate collapse.
3. **Theorem C — Longness (Lower Bound):** A basic arithmetic law — the product formula, which says that a nonzero integer cannot simultaneously be tiny in ordinary size and divisible by huge prime powers — sets a rigid floor on the combined volume (determinant) of any spanning set of integral vectors. Theorem C proves that any spanning family of integral target functions has total volume at least a baseline called the "main amount."

When Theorems A, B, and C meet inside the main inequality, the large baseline ("main amount") cancels out on both sides. What survives is an upper ceiling on $m$ times the prime remainder: if the prime remainder $R_{q_0}(x)$ ever grew larger than roughly $x^{-1/2 + \varepsilon}$, the short spanning vectors from Theorems A and B would pack strictly less volume than the arithmetic floor enforced by Theorem C — a contradiction.

To build those three ingredients without undefined gaps, the formalization relies on four deeper machines treated here as honestly typed input/output black boxes (unpacked stage by stage in Stages `01`–`07`):

- **Theta derivatives and formal groups (`02_derivatives`):** *Input:* a cyclotomic CM field $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d}, \zeta_{q_1})$ and derivative orders up to $a = x b$. *Output:* exact $p$-adic divisibility bounds on Taylor coefficients ("jets") of theta functions that count inert primes weighted by $\Lambda(n)(1 - \chi_K(n))$.
- **Composite theta lifts and seesaw identities (`03_upper_bound`, `04_spanning`):** *Input:* algebraic modular forms on definite unitary groups and Poincaré series translates. *Output:* an $m$-step chain map whose divisibility and archimedean size establish Theorem A, paired with a second-moment estimate establishing Theorem B.
- **Single-lift volume heights and gluing (`05_determinant`, `06_one_lift`):** *Input:* regular families of anticyclotomic twists of the $m$-step chain. *Output:* exact determinant factorization and height bounds for the cost of one lift, overcoming a naive $2/3$ exponent barrier via twist averaging and gluing maps.
- **Galois representations, Selmer groups, and $p$-adic lifting (`07_congruences`):** *Input:* Hecke eigensystems and congruence data at auxiliary primes. *Output:* existence of regular $m$-fold twist families whose congruence denominators stay small enough to keep Theorem C's lower bound sharp.

Together with the Stage 00 contract checked by {{concept:formal-verification|machine-checking every logical step in a computer kernel against explicit axioms}}, these eight pillars form one funnel from prime-counting mechanics at the bottom to the five headline theorems at the top.

### Where this analogy breaks

Thinking of Theorems A, B, and C as packing short sticks into a box whose volume cannot shrink below an arithmetic floor captures the exact cancellation logic of `star_inequality`. The analogy breaks in two decisive ways. First, there is no single Euclidean box: the "shortness" and "longness" bounds live simultaneously across ordinary complex norms (archimedean places) and $p$-adic valuations at every prime $\ell$, linked together by a twisted-lattice height formalism where the denominator-clearing matrix varies across primes while staying independent of the twist. Second, the chain of $m \approx \sqrt{x}$ lifts does not simply multiply one number $m$ times; without twisting and congruence control from Pillars `05`–`07`, the "cost" of each lift would stall at an exponent of $2/3$ instead of reaching $1/2$.
